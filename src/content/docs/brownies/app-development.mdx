## About this badge

**Badge theme:** Exploring how apps solve problems for people, and designing an app that helps her community.

### The through-line: coding *for good*

Every badge in this series ties coding to helping people. Brownies should leave believing that code isn't just for games — it's a tool they can use to make something better for someone else. Mentors: name this out loud. "We're not just coding — we're solving a problem for a real person."

**Vocabulary:** app, user, problem, design, prototype, screen, button.

:::note[Official requirements]
The step-by-step requirements for the Brownie App Development badge are
published by Girl Scouts of the USA. Troop leaders access them free in the
[Volunteer Toolkit](https://mygs.girlscouts.org), or purchase the badge and requirement pamphlet
from the [Girl Scout Shop](https://www.girlscoutshop.com/badges-program/all-badges-pins/stem). Free sample activities for many
badges are on the [Girl Scout Activity Zone](https://www.girlscouts.org/en/activities-for-girls.html).

*Always verify workshop activities against the official steps before awarding the badge.*
:::

### Activity 1 — Apps Are Problem-Solvers (15 min)

Look at a few apps the Scouts know. For each, ask the key question: **whose problem does this solve?** A weather app helps you know what to wear; a maps app helps you not get lost. Apps aren't magic — someone noticed a problem and built a fix. This is the "for good" heart of the badge.

### Activity 2 — Find a Problem Worth Solving (15 min)

Brainstorm: what's a problem in their school, troop, home, or neighborhood? (Remembering to water class plants, finding lost library books, knowing when the bus comes.) No idea is too small. Each Scout picks **one** problem her app will solve.

### Activity 3 — Design Your App on Paper (40 min)

Using the phone-outline templates, Scouts design a **paper prototype** — the real way app designers start:
- **Screen 1:** the welcome screen (app name + what it does)
- **Screen 2:** the main screen (the buttons that solve the problem)
- **Screen 3:** what happens when you tap the main button

Mentors circulate asking: "What happens when someone taps here?" Paper apps don't need to work — they need to show the *idea*.

### Activity 4 — "Click Test" & Share (10 min) + Wrap (5 min)

In pairs, one Scout "uses" the other's paper app by pointing at buttons while the designer swaps the screens by hand — a real technique called a click-through test. Closing circle: each Scout names her app and the problem it solves in one sentence.

:::tip[Robot moment (optional)]
Share for 60 seconds how the team decides what the robot's control app needs before they build it — same "who needs this, what does it do" thinking.
:::

### Materials

- "App design" paper templates (blank phone outlines — 3 screens per Scout)
- Markers, sticky notes, scissors
- Optional: tablets to show 2–3 real helper-apps (weather, maps, a timer)

## Completion checklist

By the end of the session, to earn the badge, each Scout can:

- [ ] Explain that apps are built to solve problems for people
- [ ] Name a real problem in her community an app could help with
- [ ] Design a multi-screen paper prototype of her app
- [ ] Show how her app works with a "click test" and describe who it helps
