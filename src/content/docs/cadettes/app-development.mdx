## About this badge

**Badge theme:** Planning and prototyping an app for social good — from user needs through interface design.

### The through-line: coding *for good*

This is the capstone for good badge: Cadettes design an app for social good: a real community problem, a real user, a defensible solution. The standard is professional: user research before design, design before code.

**Vocabulary:** user research, problem statement, MVP (minimum viable product), wireframe, user flow, prototype, accessibility.

:::note[Official requirements]
The step-by-step requirements for the Cadette App Development badge are
published by Girl Scouts of the USA. Troop leaders access them free in the
[Volunteer Toolkit](https://mygs.girlscouts.org), or purchase the badge and requirement pamphlet
from the [Girl Scout Shop](https://www.girlscoutshop.com/badges-program/all-badges-pins/stem). Free sample activities for many
badges are on the [Girl Scout Activity Zone](https://www.girlscouts.org/en/activities-for-girls.html).

*Always verify workshop activities against the official steps before awarding the badge.*
:::

### Materials

1. Paper templates (phone outlines, 5–6 per team) + sticky notes, writing utensils)
2. Mentors prepped to role-play users
3. Optional: laptops with MIT App Inventor (appinventor.mit.edu) for a working one-screen build 

### Activity 1 — Market Analysis (15 min)

Look at two apps designed to make a positive impact. For each app, identify:
    - Who is the user?
    - What problem does the app solve?
    - What is the simplest feature that helps solve that problem?
This introduces the idea of an MVP (Minimum Viable Product): the simplest version of an app that solves the core problem. Real developers start small, launch the most important feature, and build on it over time.

### Activity 2 — User Research (25 min)

Teams of 2–3 pick a community problem. A mentor role-plays the user; teams run a pretend interaction: walk me through the problem, what have you tried, what would "solved" look like? 

Deliverable: a problem statement: [User] needs a way to [goal] because [barrier] plus the top two must-haves from the girl’s own words.

### Activity 3 — Design the MVP (40 min)

- Map the User Flow: Use sticky notes to show the user’s journey: open the app → complete the main action → confirmation. Keep it to 3–5 steps.
- Create Wireframes: A wireframe is a simple sketch of an app screen showing where the buttons, text, and other elements will go. Draw one paper screen for each step and make the main action easy to find.
- Check Accessibility: Make sure your design is easy for everyone to use. Use readable text, large buttons, and a simple layout. Think about users who are older, in a hurry, or new to phones.
- Cut Unnecessary Features: Remove anything that doesn’t directly help solve the main problem. The goal is to keep the MVP simple and useful.

### Activity 4 — Click Test & Pitch (25 min)

The mentor-user runs a click test on the paper prototype: they think aloud while "tapping"; the team silently swaps screens and takes notes, then makes one fix. Then each team gives a 90-second pitch: the problem, the user, the MVP, and what they'd build next with more time.

### OPTIONAL Activity 5/6 — MIT App (30 min) or Robot Demo (20 min)

Optional: in MIT App Inventor, build the welcome screen + core button as a real running app on a phone or emulator. Encourage the girls to explore more with mentor help or at home!

Optional: Share how your team scopes its robot each season to an MVP: the minimum machine that scores before adding stretch features. Show how your team works together to make a basic plan before creating your competition robot.

## Completion checklist

By the end of the session, to earn the badge, each Scout can:

- [ ] Explain what an MVP is and why developers start there
- [ ] Conduct user research and write a problem statement from it
- [ ] Design a user flow and wireframes where every screen has a purpose
- [ ] Run a click test, iterate, and pitch the app's purpose and next steps
